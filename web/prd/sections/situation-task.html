Situation

Sundai Hack 145 ran at MIT on 18 October 2026. Track 1 asked builders to design the AI data center of 2035: one that can serve its users fast enough while spending as little energy and money as it can. The deck frames the job as a digital twin, which is a computer model that mirrors a real facility so you can change a setting and watch what happens. This PRD describes Rack to the Future, a twin that runs from a single chip up to a fleet of sites on Earth and in orbit.

Why now

The deck opens with four facts. Together they say that power, not ideas, now limits AI.

$50 to $60Bto build one gigawatt (a thousand megawatts) of AI data center, chips included, as cited on slide 8
132 to 600 kWpower per rack, from the 2025 GB200 NVL72 to the 2027 Kyber rack. A rack is one cabinet of computer chips; a kilowatt (kW) is a thousand watts
10xrise in PJM grid capacity prices in two years, mostly from data center demand
under $200/kgprojected price to launch cargo to orbit by the mid-2030s, when space starts to compete with the ground (see also Google Research)

The setting

Every team gets the same box to work in: a $30B five-year budget for chips, buildings, energy and launches; 500 MW of grid power on Earth for all sites together (a megawatt, MW, is a million watts); and a ride to orbit at $200/kg. The fleet can use six Earth sites, low Earth orbit, the Moon and Mars. Prefill racks run on GPUs or cheap air-cooled cards. Decode racks run on GPUs or LPUs (slide 9).

Two words matter from here on. Prefill is the AI reading your prompt. Decode is the AI writing its reply one token at a time, where a token is a small chunk of text. A GPU is a general AI chip with big memory. An LPU is a chip built for fast decode: very fast memory, but not much of it (200 TB/s but only 160 GB). The score rewards users served within the constraints first, then dollars saved.

Who gets served

The workloads are fixed. A chat prompt runs on one open model. An agent task, where an AI works through many steps on its own, runs on a larger model and re-reads a very long saved context at every step (slide 10). There are three cases of users to serve.

Out means decode and in means prefill. A single agent developer reads roughly 450 prompters' worth of tokens, so a small user count can still mean a huge load.

The limits that bite

  • Time to first token (TTFT). Chat must start answering in 0.6 s, and an interactive agent in 2 s per step. Distance decides it. Round trips run from 25 ms in Northern Virginia and 45 ms in West Texas to 2.6 s for the Moon and 25 min for Mars.
  • Memory. Each new token makes the chip re-read the user's saved context, called the KV cache. One 2035 GPU has 3 TB at 120 TB/s, which fits about 960 chat users at once.
  • Power and cooling. PUE (power usage effectiveness) is total facility power divided by chip power, so a PUE near 1 means little is wasted on cooling (slide 15). Air cooling tops out at 50 kW per rack and direct liquid cooling at 1.5 MW, while a 2035 GPU rack draws 792 kW. Evaporative cooling uses 1.8 L of water for each kWh, and carbon costs $100 a tonne.
  • Orbit. Space has no grid cap, but heat can only leave through radiators: 1,080 m2 and 18 t of hardware per megawatt of GPUs. The slides say orbit ties the Nordics at about $95/kg and West Texas at about $170/kg.

How the track is scored

Slides 19 to 23 ask for a digital twin that spends as few joules per task as it can. A joule is a unit of energy. Judging weighs energy, latency, robustness and explainability (slides 19 to 23). Slides are not allowed at the demo, so what is on screen has to explain itself.

The problem this build takes on

The spec describes the gap this way. Facility choices about chips, cooling, power, buildings, cost and public reaction sit in different spreadsheets and tools. No one sees how a decision at one scale forces a decision at another. A chip that draws 5.5 kW forces liquid cooling, which forces a heavy rack, which forces a hall, a building, a grid tie and a site that locals may or may not want. A planner finds out about the grid limit late, from a different file than the one that holds the chip.

The core story. Case B needs about 1,790 racks of 2035 GPUs, around 1.4 GW of IT power (the power the chips themselves draw). Earth's grid gives 500 MW. Power gives out first, long before the budget does, and the twin should show where compute can go instead. The spec's worked example points the same way: about 631 racks fit under 500 MW before cooling overhead; with cooling, the start preset fits 575.

Task

Build one shared model of an AI data center and draw it at seven scales, so that a planner, an engineer and a first-time viewer can all see which limit binds first and why. The project card says you can "zoom from a single chip to an entire fleet of data centers and beyond." (project card)

What this build is

The idea is one scenario, one engine and one set of metrics. Every scale reads the same numbers, so a change at one scale shows up at all the others. Five lenses (heat, power, cost, sentiment and water) recolour the scene without moving the camera, or say when they do not apply at that scale; each lens is a colour code that changes what the colours mean. A year slider swaps in each year's rack catalog, 2025 to 2035, and keeps the current rack counts; only a preset or the Size to demand button re-sizes the fleet to the demand. Budget and grid power are sliders, not hard rules, and presets (saved starting points for the sliders) tell a guided story. Six checks run on every change, and the first one that fails shows as a red banner that zooms to its cause.

The spec describes a native Apple Vision Pro app. This build takes the same idea to a static web page that draws with three.js, a JavaScript 3D library. It runs in any browser. It also runs in Safari on Apple Vision Pro through WebXR, the browser standard for headset display. Where the slides are silent, the numbers come from the Terawatt app engine, and each one says so.

Two modules

Each module should be explainable in ten seconds.

Module 1: Business, map first

Pick a place, then see what it costs and who objects.

  1. Pick a region on the map and read its constraints: power price, carbon, fresh water and local sentiment (how locals feel, from one news article per site).
  2. See the facility size in MW, racks and halls.
  3. See a ghost building (a see-through outline) sized to that MW.
  4. Follow the money flow to NPV, IRR and payback. NPV (net present value) is what all future cash is worth today, IRR (internal rate of return) is the yearly return that makes NPV zero, and payback is the years until the money is back.
  5. Check the 2025 to 2035 projection.
  6. See existing data centers on the map.

Module 2: Engineering

Build a chip, raise a rack, walk a hall.

  1. Stack chips on a workbench and watch power flow and heat build up.
  2. Watch racks rise from the table.
  3. Inspect a tabletop hall in miniature.
  4. Step into a life-size portal in XR (extended reality, meaning headset view) and stand among the racks.

Jobs to be done

Three people hire this build. The spec names planning teams as the main users and lists hackathon judges among the users. These jobs follow from the spec and the track deck, not from interviews.

1. The planner: where and what to build

When I have a budget and a grid cap and must choose where and what to build, show me which limit I hit first and what it costs, so I can decide with evidence.

  • Functional. Compare regions on power price, carbon, water and local sentiment. Size the facility. See NPV, IRR, payback and the break-even price per user per month.
  • Social. Defend the choice to finance and community partners, with a source behind every number.
  • Emotional. Avoid being surprised late by the grid, the budget or a local backlash.
  • Pain. Today the answers sit in separate spreadsheets, and the assumptions are hidden.
  • Done when AC1, AC3, AC6, AC7, AC9, AC10 and AC11 pass.

2. The engineer: can it be cooled and served

When I have a chip or rack design, show me whether it can be cooled and still serve the users, so I can fix the weak link before it is built.

  • Functional. Add compute dies and memory stacks, and read chip power and heat flux (watts per square centimetre of chip). Check each rack against its cooling. Walk a hall. Compare 2025 and 2035 racks.
  • Social. Show a colleague the reasoning on one shared model instead of a slide.
  • Emotional. Feel sure nothing hot is hiding in the numbers.
  • Pain. A 2035 GPU rack draws 792 kW, far above the 50 kW an air-cooled rack can take.
  • Done when AC4, AC5, AC12 and AC14 pass.

3. The judge or member of the public: understand it in ten seconds

When I see a short demo with no slides, I want to get the point in ten seconds, so I can tell whether the design serves users with less energy.

  • Functional. See the story end to end: the grid binds, compute goes to orbit, and a price comes out.
  • Social. Explain it to the next person after one viewing.
  • Emotional. Trust that the numbers are real. Every number in the panels has an i badge that names its source.
  • Pain. Dense dashboards that need a guide. Explainability is one of the four scoring axes (slides 19 to 23).
  • Done when AC1, AC2, AC4, AC6, AC8 and AC13 pass.

The demo story, in numbers

These are start-preset readings from the engine, shown to set expectations. The app always shows live values, and they change with every slider.

32%of peak demand served by 575 racks
499 MWdrawn from a 500 MW grid cap
$23.6Bfive-year cost against a $30B budget
$34.45break-even price per user per month, beside $20 plans and plans from $100

The grid has almost no room and the budget still has room, yet only about a third of demand is served. The Serving check asks whether the racks deliver enough tokens for the whole case at peak, so it is the first one to fail. A tap on it drops the view to the decode racks at the West Texas site. Adding orbit with LPU racks lifts service to 97%, but the five-year cost rises to $39.7B and the budget check fails next. The break-even price then falls to $19.66, close to the $20 plans. Whether that trade is worth it depends on launch cost and radiator mass, which the sliders let a viewer test.

Success criteria

Success is binary: all fourteen acceptance criteria, AC1 to AC14, pass in a clean browser before the evening demo. QA runs them as Given, When, Then tests; the Action section lists them word for word. A second check is human: a first-time viewer can say the story back in ten seconds.

Out of scope

  • Detailed airflow or electrical simulation, live facility data, and an optimizer that picks the best fleet. The spec lists these as non-goals: the twin lays out what follows from a choice, and people make the choice.
  • The spec's manufacturing and physics-simulation lenses. This build has the five lenses listed above.
  • Live social media scraping. Sentiment is one recent news article per site with a stance label, which is a sample, not a poll (source note).
  • Engineering drawings. Shapes are simple on purpose. The spec names false precision as a risk, so schematic views are labelled and assumptions carry their reasons.
